Dive into Chemical Bonding

Start with the 6 big questions: Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How.

6 Big Questions

WHO Who founded the theory of Chemical Bonding? ▼

W. Kossel and G.N. Lewis (1916) provided the first successful explanation of chemical bonding based on inert gas electronic configurations. Their work was later expanded by Gillespie & Nyholm (VSEPR Theory), Linus Pauling (Valence Bond Theory & Hybridisation), and Hund & Mulliken (Molecular Orbital Theory).

⭐ Fun Fact: Linus Pauling won two Nobel Prizes — Chemistry (1954) for his work on chemical bonding, and Peace (1962). He is the only person to win two unshared Nobel Prizes.
WHAT What is Chemical Bonding? ▼

Chemical bonding is the attractive force holding atoms, ions, or molecules together to form stable chemical species with lower overall potential energy. The three primary types are ionic bonds (electron transfer), covalent bonds (electron sharing), and metallic bonds (electron sea model).

⭐ Fun Fact: The concept of 'bond' was introduced long before electrons were even discovered. The electron was discovered in 1897 by J.J. Thomson, but Berzelius proposed chemical bonding in 1819!
WHEN When do atoms form chemical bonds? ▼

Atoms combine when the net attractive forces between nuclei and valence electrons exceed electrostatic repulsive forces, leading to a minimum energy state. The resulting molecule is more stable than the individual atoms.

⭐ Fun Fact: Bond formation is purely spontaneous when it leads to energy minimization — no external force is needed.
WHERE Where does Chemical Bonding occur? ▼

Bonding occurs in the valence shell electronic orbitals of atoms during chemical reactions, forming ionic lattices, covalent molecules, or metallic networks. The spatial arrangement depends on the type of bond and orbital interactions.

⭐ Fun Fact: Chemical bonding happens in the outermost shell of an atom — the inner shell electrons (core electrons) almost never participate in bonding.
WHY Why do atoms form chemical bonds? ▼

Atoms bond to achieve a stable octet (8 electrons in valence shell) matching noble gas configurations, lowering system potential energy. This is known as the Octet Rule, first proposed by Lewis.

⭐ Fun Fact: Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe) have full valence shells and almost never form bonds naturally.
HOW How are chemical bonds formed? ▼

By transfer of electrons (Ionic bonding), sharing of electron pairs (Covalent bonding), coordinate/dative sharing (one atom provides both electrons), or delocalized molecular orbital formation (MOT). Each mechanism leads to distinct bond properties.

⭐ Fun Fact: The 'sharing' in covalent bonds is often unequal — electrons spend more time near the more electronegative atom, creating polar covalent bonds.

Lewis Structures & Octet Rule

Master drawing Lewis dot structures, assigning formal charges, and identifying resonance forms.

Lewis dot symbols Formal charge Resonance Exceptions to Octet

📖 Key Concepts — Lewis Structures

  • Lewis Dot Symbols: Represent valence electrons as dots around the element symbol. E.g., Na has 1 dot, Cl has 7 dots.
  • Octet Rule: Atoms tend to achieve 8 electrons in their valence shell (except H which needs 2). This drives bond formation.
  • Drawing Lewis Structures: Count total valence electrons → arrange atoms → place bonding pairs → distribute lone pairs → check octets.
  • Formal Charge: FC = V − N − B/2. The structure with lowest formal charges is most stable.
  • Resonance: When a single Lewis structure can't explain bonding, multiple equivalent structures (resonance structures) are drawn. The actual structure is a hybrid.
  • Exceptions: Incomplete octet (BF₃, AlCl₃), expanded octet (PCl₅, SF₆), odd-electron molecules (NO, NO₂).

VSEPR & Molecular Geometry

Predict 3D shapes of molecules using repulsion of electron pairs.

BP vs LP repulsion AXₙEₘ notation Bond angles Dipole moment

📖 Key Concepts — VSEPR Theory

  • VSEPR: Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion theory — electron pairs around a central atom repel each other and adopt geometries that minimize repulsion.
  • Repulsion Order: LP–LP > LP–BP > BP–BP. Lone pairs occupy more space and compress bond angles.
  • AXₙEₘ Notation: A = central atom, X = bonded atoms, E = lone pairs. E.g., H₂O = AX₂E₂ → Bent shape.
  • Common Geometries: AX₂ = Linear (180°), AX₃ = Trigonal Planar (120°), AX₄ = Tetrahedral (109.5°), AX₅ = Trigonal Bipyramidal, AX₆ = Octahedral (90°).
  • Dipole Moment: Vector sum of individual bond dipoles. Symmetric molecules (CO₂, BF₃, CH₄) have μ = 0.

Hybridization & Orbital Overlap

Understand sp, sp², sp³, sp³d, sp³d² hybridization, σ & π bond formation.

sp, sp², sp³ σ & π bonds VBT concepts MOT & Bond Order

📖 Key Concepts — Hybridization & MOT

  • Hybridization: Intermixing of atomic orbitals to form new hybrid orbitals of equal energy. sp (linear, 180°), sp² (trigonal planar, 120°), sp³ (tetrahedral, 109.5°).
  • Extended Hybridization: sp³d (trigonal bipyramidal, 5 regions), sp³d² (octahedral, 6 regions). Requires d-orbitals, possible from Period 3 onwards.
  • σ (Sigma) Bond: Head-on overlap of orbitals along the internuclear axis. Always the first bond formed. Free rotation possible.
  • π (Pi) Bond: Sideways/lateral overlap of unhybridized p-orbitals above and below the internuclear axis. Found in double and triple bonds.
  • MOT: Atomic orbitals combine to form bonding MOs (lower energy, stabilizing) and antibonding MOs (higher energy, destabilizing). Bond Order = ½(Nb − Na).
  • Paramagnetism: O₂ has 2 unpaired electrons in π* antibonding MOs → paramagnetic. MOT explains this; VBT cannot.

Key Identities & Formulas

Essential formulas for Chemical Bonding calculations.

1. Formal Charge

FC = V − N − B/2
V = Valence electrons of the free atom
N = Non-bonding (lone pair) electrons on the atom
B = Bonding electrons around the atom
Example: In CO₂, FC on Carbon = 4 − 0 − 8/2 = 0
FC on each Oxygen = 6 − 4 − 4/2 = 0

2. Bond Order (MOT)

BO = ½(Nb − Na)
Nb = Number of electrons in bonding MOs
Na = Number of electrons in antibonding MOs
Higher BO → shorter bond, greater stability
Example: O₂: Nb=10, Na=6 → BO = ½(10−6) = 2
N₂: Nb=10, Na=4 → BO = ½(10−4) = 3

3. Dipole Moment

μ = Q × r
Q = Magnitude of charge (in C or esu)
r = Distance between charges (in cm or m)
Unit: Debye (D) = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m
Example: HCl → μ = 1.07 D
H₂O → μ = 1.85 D (bent geometry, lone pairs)

4. % Ionic Character

% Ionic = (μ_observed / μ_calculated) × 100
μ_observed = Experimentally measured dipole moment
μ_calculated = Dipole moment assuming 100% ionic bond
Example: HCl → μ_obs = 1.07D, μ_calc = 6.12D → 17.2% ionic

The Ultimate Mind Map

Deep dive into every sub-topic. Click nodes to expand/collapse.

  • Chemical Bonding & Molecular Structure
    • 1. Kossel-Lewis Approach (Classical)
      • Octet Rule
        • Definition: Atoms transfer or share e⁻ to get 8 valence e⁻
        • Exceptions: Incomplete Octet (BF₃, BeCl₂), Expanded Octet (PCl₅, SF₆, H₂SO₄), Odd e⁻ (NO, NO₂)
      • Electrovalent (Ionic) Bond
        • Formation: Complete transfer of electron(s) from metal to non-metal
        • Factors Favoring: Low IE (metal), High negative EGE (non-metal), High Lattice Enthalpy
        • Born-Haber Cycle: Relates sublimation, ionization, dissociation, electron gain, and lattice enthalpies
      • Formal Charge: FC = V - N - B/2 (Evaluates stability of Lewis structures)
    • 2. Covalent Bond & Parameters
      • Bond Parameters
        • Bond Length: Equilibrium distance between nuclei. Decreases with bond multiplicity
        • Bond Angle: Angle between bonds around central atom (measured via X-ray)
        • Bond Enthalpy: Energy to break one mole of bonds. Relates to bond strength
        • Bond Order: No. of bonds between two atoms (Single=1, Double=2, Triple=3)
      • Resonance
        • Concept: Multiple Lewis structures (canonical forms) stabilizing a molecule (e.g., CO₃²⁻, O₃)
        • Resonance Energy: Difference in energy between actual hybrid and most stable canonical form
      • Polarity & Dipole Moment
        • Dipole Moment (μ): μ = Q × r. Zero for highly symmetric geometries (CCl₄, BF₃, BeF₂)
        • Fajans' Rules: Predicts covalent character in ionic bonds (Small Cation, Large Anion = high covalent)
    • 3. Modern Quantum Mechanical Theories
      • VSEPR Theory
        • Core Postulate: Electron pairs repel each other. Repulsion order: lp-lp > lp-bp > bp-bp
        • Geometry vs Shape: Geometry includes lp; Shape is determined only by atomic positions
        • Key Shapes (with lp): Bent (H₂O, SO₂), Trigonal Pyramidal (NH₃), See-Saw (SF₄), T-Shape (ClF₃), Square Planar (XeF₄)
      • Valence Bond Theory (VBT)
        • Orbital Overlap: Bonds form by partial merging of half-filled atomic orbitals (e.g., s-s, s-p, p-p overlap)
        • Sigma (σ) Bond: Head-on overlap, free rotation, stronger
        • Pi (π) Bond: Sideways overlap, restricts rotation, weaker
      • Hybridization
        • sp: Linear (180°) e.g., BeCl₂, C₂H₂
        • sp²: Trigonal Planar (120°) e.g., BF₃, C₂H₄
        • sp³: Tetrahedral (109.5°) e.g., CH₄, NH₃, H₂O
        • sp³d: Trigonal Bipyramidal (90°, 120°) e.g., PCl₅
        • sp³d²: Octahedral (90°) e.g., SF₆
      • Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT)
        • LCAO Principle: Linear combination of atomic orbitals creates MOs
        • Bonding MO: Addition of wave functions (σ, π). Lower energy, higher stability
        • Antibonding MO: Subtraction of wave functions (σ*, π*). Higher energy, nodal plane
        • Magnetic Prop: If all e⁻ paired = Diamagnetic; If unpaired e⁻ exist (like in O₂) = Paramagnetic
    • 4. Weak Intermolecular Forces
      • Hydrogen Bonding
        • Condition: H atom covalently bonded to highly electronegative atom (N, O, F)
        • Intermolecular: Between different molecules (Raises boiling point: H₂O, HF, NH₃)
        • Intramolecular: Within same molecule (Chelation effect: o-nitrophenol)

High Yield NCERT Concepts & Traps

High-probability questions, subtle NCERT exceptions, and common examination pitfalls.

⚡ High Yield #1: MOT Bond Order & Diamagnetic/Paramagnetic Species
Core NCERT Fact: Liquid O₂ is paramagnetic with 2 unpaired electrons in degenerate (π*2px)¹ (π*2py)¹ orbitals.
⚠️ Examiner Pitfall / Trap:
NEET Trap: F₂ and O₂²⁻ both have 18 electrons and Bond Order = 1.0 (Diamagnetic). Do not confuse O₂ (16 e⁻, paramagnetic, B.O.=2) with O₂²⁻ (18 e⁻, diamagnetic, B.O.=1).
⚡ High Yield #2: VSEPR Shapes with Lone Pairs
Core NCERT Fact: Repulsion hierarchy: lp-lp > lp-bp > bp-bp. Lone pairs occupy equatorial positions in TBP (sp3d) to minimize 90° repulsions.
⚠️ Examiner Pitfall / Trap:
NEET Trap: ClF₃ has 3 bond pairs and 2 lone pairs → T-shaped (NOT trigonal planar). XeF₄ has 4 bond pairs and 2 lone pairs → Square planar (NOT tetrahedral).
⚡ High Yield #3: Isoelectronic Species Rule
Core NCERT Fact: Species with identical total electron counts have identical Bond Orders and similar MO configurations.
⚠️ Examiner Pitfall / Trap:
NEET Trap: N₂ (14 e⁻), CO (14 e⁻), NO⁺ (14 e⁻), CN⁻ (14 e⁻) ALL have Bond Order = 3.0 and are Diamagnetic.
⚡ High Yield #4: Dipole Moment — NH₃ vs NF₃
Core NCERT Fact: Dipole moment of NH₃ (1.47 D) is much greater than NF₃ (0.23 D).
⚠️ Examiner Pitfall / Trap:
In NH₃, the orbital dipole of lone pair reinforces N-H bond dipoles. In NF₃, fluorine is more electronegative than nitrogen, so N-F dipoles oppose the lone pair dipole!
⚡ High Yield #5: Hydrogen Bonding Boiling Point Anomalies
Core NCERT Fact: Intermolecular H-bonding increases boiling point and water solubility (e.g., H₂O, ethanol, p-nitrophenol).
⚠️ Examiner Pitfall / Trap:
NEET Trap: o-Nitrophenol has Intramolecular H-bonding (chelation) → Steam volatile, lower boiling point than p-nitrophenol.
